    2026-11-09 Longevity Research Update

    Educational research support only; not medical advice. Items were screened against the repository selection rubric and medical-safety policy. The scan prioritized clinically meaningful human evidence relevant to a 74-year-old male.

    Search notes

    • Window searched: 2026-10-26 through 2026-11-09, with emphasis on items published or newly indexed after the 2026-11-02 update.
    • Sources checked: PubMed/NCBI E-utilities using both publication-date and Entrez-indexing windows, configured Nature Aging/Aging Cell/GeroScience/NIA feeds, arXiv, Semantic Scholar, Crossref, and publisher metadata where needed.
    • Source limitations: Semantic Scholar returned HTTP 429 for all four searches. The NIA RSS endpoint returned HTTP 405, and the configured GeroScience RSS response was malformed; PubMed and Crossref were used to verify relevant journal items. The arXiv searches found no persuasive human clinical intervention study in the window. Nature Aging and Aging Cell feed items were predominantly mechanistic, preclinical, or non-human and were not promoted into the clinical update.
    • A newly highlighted large nicotinamide-riboside Parkinson trial was screened but not treated as a within-window paper because the primary JAMA report was published on 2026-10-08. It remains relevant background for the existing NAD-precursor caution but was not counted as a new accepted item in this scan.

    Accepted items

    1) Statin discontinuation was associated with more hospitalization in fitter older adults, but not in severe frailty

    • Citation / URL: Seeley AE, van der Veen R, Wang A, et al. Efficacy and safety of statin discontinuation in patients 65 years and older according to frailty status: a population-wide retrospective cohort study in England using Clinical Practice Research Datalink data. The Lancet Healthy Longevity. 2026;100920. DOI: 10.1016/j.lanhl.2026.100920. PMID: 42971235.
    • Date found: 2026-11-09.
    • Evidence type: Population-wide retrospective matched cohort with inverse-probability weighting and competing-risk analysis.
    • Population: 197,180 weighted participants from an eligible English cohort of 383,342 persistent statin users aged ≥65; mean age 75.9 years, approximately half male, stratified by electronic frailty index and living situation.
    • Rubric score: 9.0/10.
    • Summary: The study compared all-cause hospitalization after statin discontinuation versus continuation and examined whether associations differed by frailty, dementia, care-home residence, or housebound status.
    • Main finding: Discontinuation was associated with higher hospitalization risk in the overall cohort at one year (sHR 1.22; absolute difference 37.6 per 1,000) and five years (sHR 1.11). The association diminished as frailty increased and was not higher in severe frailty or dementia; care-home residents who discontinued had lower observed hospitalization, although causal interpretation is especially uncertain in these highly selected groups.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: This near-age-matched evidence argues against using chronological age alone as a reason to discontinue an established statin. It also supports explicitly incorporating frailty, prognosis, treatment indication, adverse effects, goals, and care setting into deprescribing discussions.
    • Limitations: Observational design; residual confounding and confounding by indication/prognosis; discontinuation inferred from prescriptions; hospitalization is not a direct measure of cardiovascular benefit or quality of life; results do not identify the best decision for an individual.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: Do not start, stop, or restart a statin based on this study alone. Clinician review should cover primary versus secondary prevention, prior cardiovascular disease, LDL/ApoB risk, muscle symptoms, interactions, liver/kidney context, frailty trajectory, life expectancy, treatment burden, and personal goals.
    • Evidence-map change: Yes—deprescribing nuance added. Statin continuation or discontinuation in later life should be individualized rather than driven by age alone, with severe frailty and care-setting evidence remaining observational and vulnerable to selection effects.

    2) Exercise plus nutrition ranked best for selected sarcopenia outcomes, while exercise was more consistent for physical performance

    • Citation / URL: Yang H, Wang Q, Wang B, et al. Exercise and Nutritional Interventions for Muscle Strength, Physical Performance, and Body Composition Among Older Adults With Sarcopenia or Pre-Sarcopenia: A Network Meta-Analysis. Geriatrics & Gerontology International. 2026;26(11). DOI: 10.1111/ggi.70897. PMID: 42980314.
    • Date found: 2026-11-09.
    • Evidence type: Network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials with RoB 2 and CINeMA certainty assessment.
    • Population: 57 RCTs reported in 65 papers, totaling 6,017 older adults with sarcopenia or pre-sarcopenia.
    • Rubric score: 8.7/10.
    • Summary: Exercise, nutrition, combined exercise-plus-nutrition, and control strategies were compared for strength, mobility, muscle mass, and body-composition outcomes.
    • Main finding: Combined intervention ranked highest for handgrip strength and appendicular skeletal muscle mass index, while exercise showed the most consistent benefit for Timed Up and Go and gait speed. Nutrition alone improved some outcomes, but overall network certainty was limited.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: Progressive exercise remains the better-supported functional core. Nutrition can be useful when intake, protein-energy adequacy, weight loss, deficiency, or recovery needs justify it, but a ranking result does not establish a universal supplement package.
    • Limitations: Heterogeneous sarcopenia definitions, exercise prescriptions, nutritional products, duration, and baseline status; indirect network comparisons; rankings can overstate small or uncertain differences; limited evidence for falls, disability, hospitalization, or survival.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: Exercise and nutrition plans should be individualized for kidney function, diabetes, heart failure, osteoporosis, swallowing/dental issues, unintentional weight loss, medication interactions, fall risk, orthopedic limitations, and recovery capacity. Major changes warrant clinician and qualified exercise/dietetic input.
    • Evidence-map change: Yes—minor strengthening and refinement. Combined exercise and nutrition may improve selected muscle outcomes in diagnosed sarcopenia, but exercise remains more consistently supported for physical performance and the overall certainty is limited.

    3) Resistance training in frailty may improve strength and gait speed, but certainty is low and frailty-score effects remain unclear

    • Citation / URL: Qian X, Huang W, Yao L. Effects of resistance training on frailty-related outcomes in frail and pre-frail older adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Frontiers in Public Health. 2026;14:1896512. DOI: 10.3389/fpubh.2026.1896512. PMID: 42991260.
    • Date found: 2026-11-09.
    • Evidence type: Systematic review and meta-analysis of resistance-training RCTs with GRADE assessment.
    • Population: Nine trials, 27 study arms, and 566 adults aged ≥60 who were explicitly classified as frail or prefrail.
    • Rubric score: 8.0/10.
    • Summary: The review isolated structured resistance training rather than mixed exercise and assessed knee extensor strength, grip strength, gait speed, Timed Up and Go, SPPB, and frailty scores.
    • Main finding: Resistance training may improve knee extensor strength and may improve grip strength and gait speed. Evidence was low to very-low certainty, heterogeneity was substantial, clinical-importance thresholds were crossed by confidence intervals for several outcomes, and effects on TUG, SPPB, and frailty score remained uncertain.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: The result supports resistance training as an important function-preservation tool even when prefrailty or frailty is present, while emphasizing that supervision, dosing, and progression matter and that reversal of frailty itself is not established.
    • Limitations: Small total sample; heterogeneous frailty criteria and training protocols; short follow-up; uncertain clinical importance; limited reporting of falls, injuries, adherence, disability, and long-term outcomes.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: Frailty, dizziness, cardiovascular symptoms, severe osteoporosis, neuropathy, recent falls, pain, and orthopedic disease can alter safe exercise selection. Clinician or physiotherapy/exercise-professional assessment may be appropriate before major progression.
    • Evidence-map change: No confidence-level change. Resistance training remains strongly supported overall, with frail-specific outcome evidence still less certain than evidence in broader older populations.

    4) Physical activity improved subjective sleep quality in older adults, but exercise-type rankings are indirect

    • Citation / URL: Oh SE, Choe JP, Kang M. Effectiveness of Different Types of Physical Activity Interventions for Enhancing Sleep Quality in Older Adults: A Systematic Review and Network Meta-Analysis. Journal of Physical Activity and Health. Published online 2026 Nov 2:1-8. DOI: 10.1123/jpah.2026-0106. PMID: 42976814.
    • Date found: 2026-11-09.
    • Evidence type: Bayesian network meta-analysis.
    • Population: 39 studies and 3,198 adults aged ≥60; sleep quality was measured with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index.
    • Rubric score: 8.0/10.
    • Summary: Aerobic, resistance, balance, flexibility, and multicomponent activity were compared for subjective sleep-quality change.
    • Main finding: Balance training had the largest estimated improvement versus control, while aerobic, multicomponent, and resistance exercise also improved sleep scores. The apparent hierarchy relies partly on indirect comparisons and does not prove that balance exercise is the best treatment for insomnia.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: Regular activity may provide a sleep-quality benefit in addition to mobility and cardiometabolic effects. Exercise should complement, not replace, evaluation of persistent insomnia, sleep apnea, restless legs, nocturia, pain, mood, medication effects, or circadian disruption.
    • Limitations: Subjective sleep outcome; heterogeneous study quality and interventions; sparse evidence for some nodes; no direct evidence here for dementia, cardiovascular events, falls, or mortality; network ranking can be unstable.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: New exercise should account for fall, cardiac, orthopedic, and recovery risks. Persistent sleepiness, witnessed apneas, nocturnal breathing difficulty, unusual movements, or marked insomnia warrant clinical assessment rather than reliance on exercise alone.
    • Evidence-map change: Yes—new sleep section added at moderate confidence. Exercise can modestly improve subjective sleep quality, but it is not a substitute for diagnosing sleep disorders.

    5) Instability resistance training produced small executive-function gains in probable mild cognitive impairment

    • Citation / URL: da Silva DT, Cavalcante BR, Falck RS, et al. Efficacy of 24-week instability resistance training on cognitive function of older adults with probable mild cognitive impairment: A randomized, assessor-blinded, sham-controlled trial. The Journal of Nutrition, Health & Aging. 2026;31(1):101030. DOI: 10.1016/j.jnha.2026.101030. PMID: 42998615.
    • Date found: 2026-11-09.
    • Evidence type: Assessor-blinded, randomized, active-control trial.
    • Population: 162 physically independent adults aged ≥65 with probable MCI; mean age 70.4 years; 24 weeks of twice-weekly training.
    • Rubric score: 7.8/10.
    • Summary: Progressive resistance exercises performed on unstable surfaces were compared with low-intensity, nonprogressive functional and stretching/relaxation activity.
    • Main finding: Two executive-function measures improved modestly at 24 weeks, but most cognitive outcomes did not differ between groups. The study did not establish prevention of dementia or preservation of independence.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: Progressive resistance training may have selected cognitive benefits in addition to physical benefits, but unstable-surface training is not necessary to obtain the established strength/function advantages and may be unsuitable for some people at fall risk.
    • Limitations: Probable rather than biomarker-confirmed MCI; multiple cognitive outcomes with only a minority positive; no usual-care-only group; limited duration; no dementia, daily-function, or long-term clinical endpoint.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: Unstable-surface exercise should be supervised or adapted when balance, neuropathy, vision, vestibular function, osteoporosis, or falls are concerns. Cognitive symptoms warrant standard clinical assessment rather than self-treatment with a specific exercise device.
    • Evidence-map change: No. This is supportive but not sufficient to change the moderate cognitive-aging exercise assessment.

    6) Supervised exercise improved pain and walking capacity after osteoporotic vertebral fracture in a small RCT

    • Citation / URL: Salkin Y, Pelin Z, Yakut Y, Sarikaya PM. The Effect of Exercise Training on Individuals With Osteoporotic Vertebral Fracture: A Randomized Controlled Trial. Physiotherapy Research International. Published online 2026 Nov 7. DOI: 10.1002/pri.70377. PMID: 42998057.
    • Date found: 2026-11-09.
    • Evidence type: Single-center, open-label randomized controlled trial.
    • Population: 48 people with osteoporotic vertebral fracture; 44 completed 12 weeks of individually supervised twice-weekly exercise or usual activity.
    • Rubric score: 7.7/10.
    • Summary: Sessions emphasized spinal extensors, posture, lower-extremity strengthening, and balance with individualized progression.
    • Main finding: Exercise improved pain, six-minute walk distance, disability, depression, fear of movement, and osteoporosis-related quality of life versus usual activity. Sleep outcomes did not differ significantly.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: A prior vertebral fracture does not necessarily preclude exercise; appropriately designed rehabilitation may improve function and confidence. This trial does not show fracture prevention or bone-density improvement.
    • Limitations: Very small sample; single center; unblinded; short follow-up; no recurrent-fracture, fall, hospitalization, or long-term independence endpoint; sex-specific results were not reported.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: Vertebral fracture exercise requires clinical/physiotherapy guidance on spinal loading, flexion/twisting, pain, neurological symptoms, osteoporosis treatment, fall risk, and progression. New severe back pain, weakness, numbness, or bowel/bladder symptoms require prompt assessment.
    • Evidence-map change: Minor rehabilitation refinement only. Supervised post-fracture exercise can improve symptoms and function, but does not replace fracture-risk evaluation or indicated osteoporosis care.

    7) Intrinsic capacity predicted disability and mortality across two cohorts of adults aged 85-89

    • Citation / URL: Osawa Y, Sasaki T, Abe Y, Arai Y. Intrinsic capacity and risks of disability and mortality in octogenarians: a prospective analysis of two independent Japanese cohorts. The Journal of Frailty & Aging. 2026;15(6):100230. DOI: 10.1016/j.tjfa.2026.100230. PMID: 42986748.
    • Date found: 2026-11-09.
    • Evidence type: Prospective analysis replicated in two independent cohorts.
    • Population: 942 and 393 community-dwelling Japanese adults aged 85-89.
    • Rubric score: 8.3/10.
    • Summary: A five-domain intrinsic-capacity score was related to subsequent disability and mortality, with exploratory testing of added cardio-renal biomarker information.
    • Main finding: Each 10-point higher intrinsic-capacity score was associated with lower mortality in both cohorts and lower disability/care needs in some models. Disability associations weakened when death was treated as a competing event. Cardio-renal measures added modest mortality information.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: Repeated assessment of cognition, mobility, vitality, psychological status, and sensory/function domains may reveal vulnerability not captured by disease counts alone. The score is a risk marker, not a self-directed treatment target.
    • Limitations: Observational; Japanese oldest-old populations; domain construction and disability definitions differed; residual confounding; discrimination for individual prediction was not established.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: Functional decline, weight loss, cognitive change, mood symptoms, sensory loss, falls, or fatigue deserve clinical evaluation for reversible causes rather than being labeled as inevitable aging.
    • Evidence-map change: Yes—moderate risk-stratification evidence strengthened. Replication in two oldest-old cohorts supports multidomain function tracking while preserving individual-prediction limits.

    8) A large phase 3 RSV vaccine trial showed efficacy, but the investigational product was discontinued

    • Citation / URL: Comeaux CA, Testa M, Gymnopoulou E, et al. A randomised, double-blind, multicentre, placebo-controlled phase 3 efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity study of an Ad26.RSV.preF/RSV prefusion F-protein vaccine for the prevention of respiratory syncytial virus-mediated lower respiratory tract disease in adults aged 60 years and older. EBioMedicine. 2026;133:106513. DOI: 10.1016/j.ebiom.2026.106513. PMID: 42981739.
    • Date found: 2026-11-09.
    • Evidence type: Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multinational phase 3 trial.
    • Population: 23,555 vaccinated adults aged ≥60 across 14 countries; mean age 70.2 years and 46% male.
    • Rubric score: 8.7/10 as infection-resilience evidence, with limited practical applicability because the product was discontinued.
    • Summary: The EVERGREEN trial tested a single-dose Ad26 vector plus prefusion-F protein vaccine against RSV-mediated lower respiratory tract disease.
    • Main finding: Vaccine efficacy was 72.7% over a mean 234-day follow-up, based on 17 versus 31 RSV-LRTD events. Local and systemic reactions were more frequent with vaccine, while serious adverse events were similar. Development was discontinued in March 2023 for a sponsor strategic decision, so this is not an available product-choice result.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: The trial reinforces that RSV vaccination can prevent clinically relevant lower-respiratory disease in older adults, but it does not determine which currently available vaccine is appropriate or alter local eligibility guidance.
    • Limitations: Few primary endpoint events; one-season follow-up; sponsor-funded; product-specific platform; development discontinued; no direct comparison with authorized RSV vaccines; limited evidence for hospitalization, death, durability, or rare adverse events.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: Vaccine decisions should follow current jurisdiction-specific guidance, available products, comorbidities, prior reactions, timing, and co-administration plans. This trial is not a reason to seek an unavailable investigational vaccine.
    • Evidence-map change: No confidence-level change. Infection-resilience vaccination remains strong, while practical decisions depend on currently authorized products and guidance.

    9) Rapamycin exposure was not associated with longer survival in a large kidney-transplant cohort

    • Citation / URL: Stewart DE, Fitzsimmons WE. Rapamycin and human longevity among kidney transplant recipients surviving the first year. GeroScience. Published online 2026 Nov 5. DOI: 10.1007/s11357-026-02643-8. PMID: 42989006.
    • Date found: 2026-11-09.
    • Evidence type: National transplant-registry observational cohort with propensity-score weighting.
    • Population: 4,885 kidney-transplant recipients prescribed rapamycin and 6,589 not prescribed rapamycin, all alive with a functioning graft at one year; median follow-up 11 years.
    • Rubric score: 7.6/10 as an important geroscience caution item.
    • Summary: The study used a large chronic-exposure human dataset to compare survival-age distributions after adjustment for demographic and clinical differences.
    • Main finding: Adjusted median survival age was 64.6 years with rapamycin and 64.3 years without it; the confidence interval excluded a large survival difference but remained compatible with modest benefit or harm. No meaningful longevity advantage was observed.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: This weakens claims that chronic human rapamycin exposure already has observational evidence of lifespan extension. It does not answer whether different doses or schedules could benefit generally healthy older adults.
    • Limitations: Highly selected transplant population with major disease, immunosuppression, indication/channeling bias, survivor selection at one year, historical regimen differences, and no randomization; transplant dosing and risks do not map directly to experimental geroscience dosing.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: This does not support self-directed or off-label rapamycin use for longevity. Potential concerns include infection and immune effects, mouth ulcers, lipids, glucose, blood counts, kidney/liver context, wound healing, interactions, and uncertain dosing. Any discussion belongs with an experienced clinician or a regulated trial.
    • Evidence-map change: Watchlist caution strengthened, status unchanged. Human longevity evidence remains early and non-definitive; a large disease-specific cohort did not show longer survival.

    Evidence-map / watchlist disposition

    • Statin deprescribing: age alone is not a sufficient reason to stop; observational associations with hospitalization differ substantially by frailty and care setting, requiring individualized clinician review.
    • Sarcopenia/frailty: exercise remains the functional core. Combined nutrition may improve selected muscle outcomes when clinically appropriate, but certainty is limited and resistance-training effects in established frailty remain less certain than in broader older populations.
    • Sleep: exercise has moderate evidence for improving subjective sleep quality, but persistent sleep problems still require evaluation for sleep and medical disorders.
    • Bone health: small randomized evidence supports supervised functional rehabilitation after vertebral fracture, without proving fracture prevention or replacing osteoporosis care.
    • Intrinsic capacity: replicated oldest-old cohort evidence strengthens its role as a multidomain risk marker, not an individual treatment target.
    • Vaccines: a large investigational RSV trial supports the preventive concept but does not affect product choice because the tested vaccine was discontinued.
    • Rapamycin: a large transplant cohort found no survival advantage; the geroscience status remains early and off-label self-treatment remains unsupported.
