    2026-10-26 Longevity Research Update

    Educational research support only; not medical advice. Items were screened against the repository selection rubric and medical-safety policy. The scan prioritized clinically meaningful human evidence relevant to a 74-year-old male.

    Search notes

    • Window searched: 2026-10-12 through 2026-10-25, using both publication-date and recent-PubMed-indexing searches.
    • Sources checked: PubMed/NCBI E-utilities, configured Nature Aging/Aging Cell/GeroScience/NIA feeds, arXiv, Semantic Scholar, and journal/DOI metadata.
    • Source limitations: Semantic Scholar returned HTTP 429 rate limits and the NIA RSS endpoint returned HTTP 405. Aging Cell and GeroScience feeds were reachable. The configured arXiv searches returned no clinically persuasive human intervention item for inclusion.
    • Animal, mechanistic, acute-biomarker, early vaccine-candidate, and small exploratory metformin-transcriptome studies were screened but not promoted over clinical outcomes. In particular, a 15-person metformin substudy found no gene significant after multiple-comparison correction, and an acute blood-flow-restriction study measured transient biomarkers rather than function or clinical outcomes.

    Accepted items

    1) Older-adult hypertension guideline emphasizes standardized measurement, frailty, and avoiding overtreatment

    • Citation / URL: Cheng HM, Yeh CF, Wu CC, et al. 2026 THS guidelines for the management of hypertension in older adults. Journal of the Formosan Medical Association. Published online 2026 Oct 24. DOI: 10.1016/j.jfma.2026.09.040. PMID: 42926177.
    • Date found: 2026-10-26.
    • Evidence type: Multisociety clinical guideline using PICO questions, systematic evidence appraisal, and GRADE, with 63 recommendations.
    • Population: Older adults with hypertension, including people with frailty, orthostatic hypotension, cognitive vulnerability, atrial fibrillation, multimorbidity, and other age-related complexities.
    • Rubric score: 9.0/10.
    • Summary: The guideline addresses measurement, monitoring, lifestyle, treatment targets, and pharmacotherapy specifically for older adults rather than simply applying a younger-adult framework.
    • Main finding: It strongly emphasizes validated standardized blood-pressure measurement and out-of-office monitoring, while recommending individualized treatment and explicit avoidance of overtreatment in frail older adults. Dietary sodium reduction, healthy dietary patterns, physical activity, and context-specific use of potassium-enriched salt substitutes are included among non-pharmacological measures.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: Blood-pressure control remains a high-confidence healthspan intervention, but benefit depends on measurement quality and balancing cardiovascular protection against dizziness, orthostasis, falls, kidney effects, and treatment burden.
    • Limitations: This is a Taiwan-specific guideline, so drug availability, population characteristics, and exact targets may not transfer unchanged to other jurisdictions. Some recommendations necessarily combine trial evidence with expert consensus.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: This is not a reason to change antihypertensive therapy independently. Clinician discussion may include seated and standing readings, home-monitor validation, symptoms, falls, kidney function/electrolytes, frailty, cognition, atrial fibrillation, and medication interactions. Potassium-enriched salt can be unsafe with kidney impairment or medicines that raise potassium.
    • Evidence-map change: Yes—clarification. Blood-pressure control remains strong, with greater emphasis on standardized measurement, orthostatic assessment, frailty, and avoiding overtreatment.

    2) Nutrition and sarcopenia: protein-centered strategies have the most consistent support, but synthesis is heterogeneous

    • Citation / URL: Dharmansyah D, Suparto TA, Salasa S, Hikmat R, Pratama A. Protein-Based Strategies and Mediterranean-Style Diets Show the Most Consistent Benefits for Sarcopenia in Older Adults. Journal of Nutrition. Published online 2026 Oct 21. DOI: 10.1016/j.tjnut.2026.101896. PMID: 42906854.
    • Date found: 2026-10-26.
    • Evidence type: Preregistered systematic review with narrative SWiM synthesis and GRADE-style certainty assessment.
    • Population: Adults aged ≥60 across 248 included studies: 100 RCTs, 62 cohorts, 68 cross-sectional studies, three case-control studies, three quasi-experimental studies, and 12 prior systematic reviews/meta-analyses.
    • Rubric score: 8.3/10.
    • Summary: The review integrated evidence across protein and other nutrients, oral supplements, whole dietary patterns, and gut-microbiota approaches for sarcopenia prevention or management.
    • Main finding: Protein/leucine-centered strategies paired with resistance training and correction of vitamin D deficiency had the most consistent support; Mediterranean/MIND-style patterns had moderate, largely observational support. Gut-targeted interventions remained low-to-very-low certainty. The authors cite protein ranges around 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day, but this is a synthesis of general older-adult evidence rather than a personal prescription.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: The findings reinforce combining adequate nutrition with progressive loading rather than treating supplements or microbiome products as substitutes for resistance exercise and overall dietary quality.
    • Limitations: No pooled meta-analysis; very heterogeneous sarcopenia definitions, interventions, and outcomes; “direction of effect” percentages are not treatment-success rates; inclusion of cross-sectional and prior-review evidence raises overlap and causal limitations.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: Protein quantity, vitamin D, and supplements require individual context, particularly kidney disease, kidney stones, hypercalcemia, swallowing/dental issues, appetite or weight loss, diabetes, and medication interactions. Clinician or dietitian discussion is warranted before major dietary or supplement changes.
    • Evidence-map change: Yes—minor strengthening and clarification. Adequate protein remains moderate-to-strong, best framed alongside resistance training and individualized nutrition rather than as a stand-alone longevity intervention.

    3) GLP-1 receptor agonists and bone: randomized evidence remains too sparse for reassurance

    • Citation / URL: Lai CH, Li YT, Wu TS, Wu CT. Skeletal effects of GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy in adults with overweight or obesity: a systematic review. Archives of Osteoporosis. 2026 Oct 23;21(1). DOI: 10.1007/s11657-026-01785-8. PMID: 42920024.
    • Date found: 2026-10-26.
    • Evidence type: PRISMA systematic review of eight randomized trials (n=493), assessed with RoB 2 and GRADE; narrative agent-specific synthesis without pooling.
    • Population: Adults with overweight or obesity; five trials enrolled type 2 diabetes populations. Evidence covered semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, and exenatide.
    • Rubric score: 8.4/10 as a bone-safety/watchlist item.
    • Summary: The review evaluated DXA-derived bone mineral density and bone-turnover markers, separating drugs because agents and achieved weight loss differed substantially.
    • Main finding: Certainty was very low for every agent. The single semaglutide trial and the liraglutide 3.0 mg trial—also the trials with the largest weight loss—reported reductions at some spine, hip, or whole-body BMD sites; the semaglutide trial also found increased bone resorption. These findings are a signal requiring dedicated study, not proof of a class-wide fracture effect.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: Older age increases the importance of evaluating fracture risk, prior falls/fractures, osteoporosis, nutrition, muscle strength, and weight-loss rate when a GLP-1 medicine is being considered for a standard indication.
    • Limitations: Only eight small RCTs; at most one low-risk-of-bias trial per agent; short follow-up; heterogeneous comparators and populations; no adequate fracture-outcome evidence; most participants were not specifically older men.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: This does not establish that GLP-1 medicines should be avoided or stopped. For indicated use, clinician discussion may include baseline fracture risk, weight-loss pace, protein and calcium adequacy, vitamin D status when clinically relevant, resistance/balance exercise, and whether bone-density assessment is indicated.
    • Evidence-map change: Yes—watchlist caution strengthened. Disease-specific benefits remain established, but skeletal safety during substantial weight loss is unresolved and should not be inferred from body weight alone.

    4) Exercise dose and executive function in older adults with mild cognitive impairment

    • Citation / URL: Song M, Xiong Q, Qin X. Optimizing exercise prescriptions for older adults with mild cognitive impairment: a dose-response network meta-analysis of modality-dose combinations on executive function. PeerJ. 2026;14:e21805. DOI: 10.7717/peerj.21805. PMID: 42921532.
    • Date found: 2026-10-26.
    • Evidence type: Bayesian dose-response network meta-analysis of 38 randomized trials.
    • Population: Adults aged ≥60 with mild cognitive impairment; trials measured working memory, cognitive flexibility, or inhibitory control.
    • Rubric score: 8.0/10.
    • Summary: Exercise dose was standardized as MET-minutes/week, and modality-dose combinations were compared across executive-function domains.
    • Main finding: Curves suggested the most reliable executive-function gains at moderate weekly doses, approximately 300–1,000 MET-min/week, with plateaus or modest declines at higher modeled doses. Combined exercise plus cognitive training was most consistent for working memory, while comprehensive physical training and walking/aerobic exercise were more stable for other executive domains.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: For a person with diagnosed MCI, this supports structured, sustainable exercise rather than assuming that more volume is always better. It does not establish dementia prevention.
    • Limitations: Certainty ranged from low to moderate; network and dose-response estimates rely on indirect comparisons, heterogeneous cognitive tests, and model assumptions; studies were not designed around a single common dose gradient; clinical significance and dementia incidence remain uncertain.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: Exercise plans should account for baseline fitness, falls, cardiovascular symptoms, cognition, orthopedic disease, neuropathy, and medications. A clinician and appropriately qualified exercise professional can help set safe intensity and progression.
    • Evidence-map change: Yes—minor refinement. Multidomain/exercise evidence for MCI remains moderate, with emerging low-to-moderate-certainty evidence favoring sustainable moderate doses rather than maximal volume.

    5) Sleep duration and long-term cognition: target-trial emulation finds a modest association, not proof

    • Citation / URL: Xia X, Qu G, Tian J, et al. A 6-8-Hour Nighttime Sleep Strategy and Subsequent Cognitive Performance: A Target Trial Emulation Study. Nature and Science of Sleep. 2026;18:633219. DOI: 10.2147/NSS.S633219. PMID: 42904162.
    • Date found: 2026-10-26.
    • Evidence type: Target-trial emulation using a nationally representative longitudinal cohort with propensity matching and inverse-probability weighting.
    • Population: 8,147 Chinese adults aged ≥45 in CHARLS followed from 2011 to 2020; 2,341 matched pairs were included after propensity matching.
    • Rubric score: 7.6/10.
    • Summary: Repeated self-reported nighttime sleep of 6–8 hours was compared with shorter or longer sleep strategies for nine-year cognitive performance.
    • Main finding: The 6–8-hour strategy was associated with modestly higher later cognitive scores. The weighted per-protocol estimate was 0.614 points on a 0–21 scale, and the modeled peak was around 6.7 hours. The absolute differences were small.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: Regular sleep duration and investigation of persistent sleep problems are reasonable clinician-discussion topics, but the study does not define an exact personal target or show that forcing sleep duration prevents dementia.
    • Limitations: Observational data despite target-trial methods; self-reported sleep; residual and time-varying confounding; selective attrition; broad age range; cognition rather than dementia, independence, or quality-of-life outcomes.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: Persistent insomnia, excessive sleepiness, loud snoring, witnessed apnea, nocturia, restless legs, mood symptoms, or sedating medicines warrant clinical assessment rather than self-treatment. Sleep medicines and supplements can increase confusion, falls, or interactions in older adults.
    • Evidence-map change: No confidence-level change. Adds methodologically stronger observational support for sleep regularity and evaluation, not a new treatment claim.

    6) Structured medication reviews improve prescribing more consistently than patient outcomes

    • Citation / URL: Lammila-Escalera E, Greenfield G, Aldakhil R, et al. Structured medication reviews for adults with multimorbidity and polypharmacy in primary care: a systematic review. BMJ Open. 2026;16(10):e117955. DOI: 10.1136/bmjopen-2026-117955. PMID: 42914541.
    • Date found: 2026-10-26.
    • Evidence type: Systematic review with narrative synthesis of nine studies (8,213 participants), including randomized and non-randomized designs.
    • Population: Adults with multimorbidity and polypharmacy in primary care, usually older adults.
    • Rubric score: 7.8/10 as medication-safety evidence.
    • Summary: Reviews were delivered by general practitioners, pharmacists, or multidisciplinary teams and assessed prescribing, safety, function, healthcare use, costs, and person-centered outcomes.
    • Main finding: Structured reviews more consistently changed prescriptions, reduced inappropriate prescribing, or improved adherence than they improved falls, function, mortality, healthcare use, or person-centered outcomes. Cost findings were favorable in the small number of studies reporting them.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: Medication review remains clinically sensible for polypharmacy and treatment burden, but its value should not be oversold as proven to improve longevity or function without careful implementation and follow-up.
    • Limitations: Only nine heterogeneous studies; endpoint outcomes were often secondary or underpowered; mixed study designs; one-reviewer extraction with verification; unclear in-person versus remote delivery in many studies.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: A review is not an instruction to stop medicines. Changes require the prescribing clinician or pharmacist, an indication-by-indication assessment, withdrawal/rebound planning where relevant, monitoring, and explicit follow-up.
    • Evidence-map change: Yes—clarification. Medication review remains a strong safety practice, but direct evidence for improved hard clinical endpoints is limited.

    7) Physical activity and infection-related mortality in adults with cognitive impairment

    • Citation / URL: Shin S, Kim M. Aerobic physical activity is associated with lower pneumonia and COVID-19 mortality in adults with cognitive impairment. GeroScience. Published online 2026 Oct 24. DOI: 10.1007/s11357-026-02596-y. PMID: 42925461.
    • Date found: 2026-10-26.
    • Evidence type: Nationwide retrospective cohort with registry linkage, cause-specific competing-risk analyses, and false-discovery-rate correction.
    • Population: 116,563 Korean adults with clinically confirmed mild cognitive impairment or dementia; mean follow-up 3.4 years and 5,022 deaths.
    • Rubric score: 8.0/10.
    • Summary: Self-reported aerobic activity was related to all-cause and cause-specific mortality among people with cognitive impairment.
    • Main finding: Compared with inactivity, higher activity categories were associated with lower all-cause mortality. High activity was associated with lower pneumonia mortality (subdistribution HR 0.554) and COVID-19 mortality (0.520), while lower pneumonia mortality was seen across activity levels after multiplicity correction.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: If cognitive impairment is present, maintaining achievable activity may support overall resilience and function; however, preserved health and reserve may partly explain who remains active.
    • Limitations: Observational design; self-reported, single-baseline activity; reverse causation and healthy-participant bias; no objective repeated activity measurement; cognitive-impairment cohort limits generalization to cognitively healthy adults.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: Cognitive impairment can increase navigation, adherence, fall, and judgment risks. Activity may need caregiver involvement, safe environments, and clinician/exercise-professional adaptation for cardiovascular, balance, sensory, or orthopedic limitations.
    • Evidence-map change: No confidence-level change. Reinforces aerobic activity as a healthspan platform while preserving non-causal interpretation.

    8) Dapagliflozin after TAVI: post hoc frailty subgroup suggests benefit may differ by frailty

    • Citation / URL: Gonzalez-Manzanares R, Ojeda S, Amat-Santos I, et al. Dapagliflozin After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation According to Frailty in DapaTAVI Trial. Journal of the American Heart Association. 2026;15(20):e051379. DOI: 10.1161/JAHA.126.051379. PMID: 42885795.
    • Date found: 2026-10-26.
    • Evidence type: Post hoc subgroup analysis of a multicenter randomized trial.
    • Population: 1,222 older patients after TAVI with baseline FRAIL-scale assessment; 47.2% frail, 40.3% prefrail, and 12.4% robust.
    • Rubric score: 7.8/10 as an indication-specific cardiorenal/frailty item.
    • Summary: The analysis tested whether the one-year effect of dapagliflozin on death or worsening heart failure differed by baseline frailty.
    • Main finding: Frailty predicted worse outcomes. Dapagliflozin was associated with a lower primary-outcome risk in the frail subgroup (HR 0.53), but not the prefrail or robust subgroups; the treatment-by-frailty interaction was statistically significant. No frailty-related safety interaction was detected.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: This is relevant only to the specialized setting of severe aortic stenosis after TAVI, not to general longevity use. It suggests frail patients should not automatically be assumed unable to benefit from indicated cardiorenal therapy.
    • Limitations: Post hoc subgroup analysis; few robust participants and wide confidence intervals; interaction findings can be unstable; one-year follow-up; results should not be generalized beyond post-TAVI patients meeting trial criteria.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: This is not a reason to start an SGLT2 inhibitor without a standard indication and specialist review. Monitoring considerations include volume status, blood pressure/orthostasis, kidney function, genital infection, ketoacidosis risk in susceptible settings, and peri-procedural or acute-illness medication plans.
    • Evidence-map change: Yes—minor indication-specific refinement. Adds frailty-stratified evidence after TAVI without supporting SGLT2 use as a general anti-aging drug.

    Evidence-map / watchlist disposition

    • Blood pressure: remains strong; the new guideline strengthens individualized older-adult measurement, orthostatic/frailty assessment, and overtreatment cautions.
    • Muscle/nutrition: adequate protein and Mediterranean-style dietary quality remain supportive, with resistance training central and gut-targeted strategies still investigational.
    • GLP-1 watchlist: bone effects remain unresolved; small agent-specific trials raise a BMD/resorption signal during substantial weight loss but do not establish fracture harm or a class effect.
    • Cognitive aging: exercise for MCI remains moderate evidence, with dose-response modeling favoring sustainable moderate doses; sleep evidence remains observational and modest.
    • Medication safety: structured review improves prescribing processes more consistently than hard outcomes, so review quality and follow-up matter.
    • SGLT2 inhibitors: post-TAVI frailty subgroup findings are promising but indication-specific and post hoc, not a general longevity indication.
