    Accepted items

    1) Vitamin D deficiency, supplementation, and aging-related morbidity/mortality

    • Citation / URL: Israel A, Weizman A, Israel S, Ashkenazi S, Ruppin E, Magen E. Vitamin D Deficiency, Supplementation, and Aging-Related Morbidity and Mortality Across Two Longitudinal Population Cohorts. Aging Cell. 2026 Oct. DOI: 10.1111/acel.70752. PMID: 42850752.
    • Date found: 2026-10-11.
    • Evidence type: Large longitudinal electronic-health-record cohort analysis with external validation and marginal structural modeling.
    • Population: More than 468,500 adults in Leumit Health Services with >1.67 million 25-hydroxyvitamin D measurements, plus 223,175 propensity-score-matched TriNetX pairs; matched cohorts were approximately age 54 and 65%–67% female.
    • Rubric score: 8.0/10.
    • Summary: Severe vitamin D deficiency (<10 ng/mL) was compared with sufficiency (>30 ng/mL), and pharmacy-dispensed supplementation was modeled longitudinally against mortality and aging-related outcomes.
    • Main finding: Severe deficiency was associated with higher all-cause mortality in both cohorts. Supplementation was associated with dose-dependent lower mortality and morbidity signals, including mortality HR 0.72 for the highest modeled supplementation category.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: Vitamin D status is clinically measurable and directly relevant to older-adult bone, muscle, fall, and general health risk discussions. This study strengthens the case for clinician-guided detection and correction of severe deficiency rather than using vitamin D as a speculative anti-aging supplement.
    • Limitations: Observational EHR study, not an RCT; mean age was younger than 74; women were over-represented; supplementation may proxy for healthier care engagement; residual confounding and measurement differences are likely.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: Not a reason for self-directed high-dose vitamin D. Clinician discussion should include baseline 25(OH)D, calcium, kidney function, kidney-stone history, granulomatous disease, parathyroid disease, medication interactions, and risk of hypercalcemia.
    • Evidence-map change: Minor. Reinforces clinician-guided deficiency correction and bone/muscle risk assessment, but does not change the core evidence map.

    2) Retatrutide in obesity plus type 2 diabetes: TRIUMPH-2 phase 3 trial

    • Citation / URL: Bellido V, le Roux CW, Ekinci EI, Bjornstad P, Frias JP, Giblin K, et al. Retatrutide in adults with obesity and type 2 diabetes (TRIUMPH-2): a double-blind, parallel-group, randomised, placebo-controlled, phase 3 trial. Lancet. 2026 Oct 10. DOI: 10.1016/S0140-6736(26)01861-1. PMID: 42810372.
    • Date found: 2026-10-11.
    • Evidence type: Multicenter double-blind randomized placebo-controlled phase 3 trial.
    • Population: 1,152 adults with BMI ≥27 kg/m² and type 2 diabetes; mean age 55.1; 52% male; 84% completed study drug.
    • Rubric score: 8.3/10 as a disease-specific cardiometabolic/watchlist item, not as a general longevity intervention.
    • Summary: Once-weekly retatrutide, a GIP/GLP-1/glucagon receptor agonist, was tested at 4, 9, and 12 mg versus placebo over 80 weeks.
    • Main finding: Mean body-weight change at week 80 was -11.9%, -16.8%, and -18.8% for retatrutide 4, 9, and 12 mg versus -5.1% with placebo. Gastrointestinal adverse events were more common with retatrutide; hypotension and dysesthesia were also reported more often than placebo.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: This is important for the incretin/anti-obesity watchlist because cardiometabolic risk, diabetes, obesity, sleep apnea, osteoarthritis, sarcopenic obesity, and frailty often overlap in older men. The trial population was substantially younger than 74, so geriatric transfer requires caution.
    • Limitations: Not a longevity trial; not focused on adults ≥70; long-term cardiovascular, frailty, muscle, fall/fracture, nutrition, and discontinuation outcomes remain unresolved; trial participants may be healthier and more closely monitored than typical older adults.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: This is not a recommendation to start an incretin drug. Clinician discussion should consider indication, diabetes regimen, hypoglycemia risk with concomitant agents, GI tolerance, dehydration, hypotension/orthostasis, kidney function, gallbladder/pancreatitis history, retinopathy monitoring where relevant, nutrition/protein intake, resistance exercise, lean-mass preservation, and fall risk.
    • Evidence-map change: Minor watchlist update. Strengthens disease-specific incretin evidence but does not convert retatrutide or related agents into general longevity drugs.

    3) Exercise dose/modality for BDNF and depressive symptoms in MCI

    • Citation / URL: Cheng G, Qiu J, Li S. Dose- and modality-specific effects of exercise on BDNF and depression in middle-aged and older adults with mild cognitive impairment: A Bayesian network meta-analysis. PLOS One. 2026. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0358178. PMID: 42842530.
    • Date found: 2026-10-11.
    • Evidence type: Bayesian network dose-response meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.
    • Population: 28 RCTs with 2,262 adults aged ≥50 with mild cognitive impairment.
    • Rubric score: 8.0/10.
    • Summary: The review modeled exercise modality and weekly dose effects on peripheral BDNF and depressive symptoms in adults with MCI.
    • Main finding: Exercise was associated with increased peripheral BDNF and improved depressive symptoms. The quadratic model predicted peak effects around 440 MET-min/week for BDNF and 670 MET-min/week for depressive symptoms, but sensitivity analyses showed that these should be treated as research-generating dose estimates rather than clinical optima.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: Supports exercise as a cognitive-aging and mood-resilience intervention in an older-adult-risk group, while reinforcing that dose should be individualized rather than chased as a biomarker target.
    • Limitations: BDNF is a surrogate marker; MCI subtypes and exercise protocols varied; dose-response estimates depended on model form; dementia incidence, independence, hospitalization, and long-term adherence were not established.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: Exercise planning for MCI should account for fall risk, cardiovascular symptoms, medication-related dizziness or hypoglycemia, orthopedic pain, supervision needs, and realistic adherence. Do not treat BDNF as a consumer longevity target.
    • Evidence-map change: No material change. Reinforces already-strong exercise and moderate cognitive-resilience evidence.

    4) Qigong for cognition in older adults with mild cognitive impairment

    • Citation / URL: Alashram AR. Effects of qigong exercises on cognitive functioning in older adults with mild cognitive impairment: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Applied Neuropsychology: Adult. 2026 Oct 8. DOI: 10.1080/23279095.2026.2743827. PMID: 42847483.
    • Date found: 2026-10-11.
    • Evidence type: Systematic review and meta-analysis of trials.
    • Population: 21 trials with 1,429 older adults with mild cognitive impairment.
    • Rubric score: 7.8/10.
    • Summary: The meta-analysis assessed qigong-based exercise effects on global cognition, memory, and executive function.
    • Main finding: Pooled results favored qigong for global cognition, memory, and executive-function measures. The author concluded that longer-term trials are needed to confirm efficacy.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: Qigong may be a lower-impact movement option for balance, attention, stress regulation, and cognitive engagement, especially when high-intensity exercise is limited by joint pain or deconditioning.
    • Limitations: Trial heterogeneity, potential publication/language bias, variable control groups, uncertain durability, and limited hard outcomes such as dementia incidence, falls, disability, or hospitalization.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: Generally lower impact, but still requires fall-risk awareness. Dizziness, neuropathy, severe balance impairment, orthostatic hypotension, visual impairment, and musculoskeletal pain should shape supervision and progression.
    • Evidence-map change: No material change. Adds modality nuance under exercise/cognitive-aging, not a stand-alone disease-modifying intervention.

    5) Loneliness intervention in older adults: telephone narrative therapy trial

    • Citation / URL: Jiang D, Tang VFY, Chow EO, Kahlon M, Yeung DY, Aubrey R, et al. Lay counsellor-delivered wisdom-enhancement narrative-therapy and empathy-focused interventions to reduce loneliness among older adults: A three-arm randomized controlled trial. International Psychogeriatrics. 2026 Oct 8. DOI: 10.1016/j.inpsyc.2026.100299. PMID: 42850158.
    • Date found: 2026-10-11.
    • Evidence type: One-year, three-arm, assessor-blinded randomized controlled trial.
    • Population: 287 older adults in Hong Kong; mean age 76.2; 71.8% female.
    • Rubric score: 7.6/10.
    • Summary: Lay counsellor-delivered telephone wisdom-enhancement narrative therapy and empathy-focused intervention were compared with an active control for loneliness and related secondary outcomes.
    • Main finding: No overall between-group difference was found on the UCLA Loneliness Scale, but narrative therapy improved De Jong Gierveld loneliness at 6 and 12 months and improved sleep quality at 12 months.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: Social connection, loneliness, sleep, and mood are healthspan-relevant, especially because isolation can interact with frailty, cognition, adherence, and mortality risk. This trial supports scalable psychosocial interventions as clinician/community-discussion topics.
    • Limitations: Mixed loneliness-scale results; Hong Kong context; majority female sample; not powered for morbidity, cognition, hospitalization, or mortality; intervention fidelity and cultural transferability need consideration.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: Loneliness interventions should not substitute for assessment of depression, grief, cognitive decline, sleep disorders, substance use, elder abuse, or suicidality when present. Clinician or mental-health referral is warranted for significant mood symptoms or safety concerns.
    • Evidence-map change: No material change. Adds social-healthspan nuance but does not alter core intervention confidence.

    6) Immune aging single-cell atlas and health trajectories

    • Citation / URL: Terekhova M, Kleverov M, Francis T, Usoltsev D, Bohacova P, Arthur L, et al. Single-cell analyses of global cohorts outline determinants of immune aging and link the ratio of GZMK+ to GZMB+ Tem cells to health trajectories. Immunity. 2026 Oct 9. DOI: 10.1016/j.immuni.2026.09.012. PMID: 42854692.
    • Date found: 2026-10-11.
    • Evidence type: Integrated single-cell immune profiling across cohorts plus proteomic association analyses.
    • Population: 2,609 ostensibly healthy individuals across diverse ages, ancestries, and biological sexes, integrating seven public PBMC datasets and a newly generated cohort.
    • Rubric score: 7.5/10 as a biomarker/watchlist item.
    • Summary: The study mapped age-associated remodeling across immune cell populations and linked CD8+ effector-memory T-cell patterns to healthy versus pathology-associated aging trajectories.
    • Main finding: A GZMK+/GZMB+ effector-memory T-cell ratio and related plasma signature tracked immune aging heterogeneity and associated with inflammaging, poor self-reported health, all-cause mortality, and immune-related, metabolic, and cardiovascular disease risk.
    • Relevance to a 74-year-old male: Helps explain why immune aging is heterogeneous and why infection resilience, CMV/chronic viral exposure, vaccination response, inflammation, and cardiometabolic disease may cluster differently across older adults.
    • Limitations: Biomarker and mechanistic association study; not an intervention trial; single-cell immune signatures are not routine clinical tests; causality and actionable thresholds are not established.
    • Safety / clinician cautions: Do not use commercial immune-age or inflammaging tests to self-direct treatment. Practical infection-resilience discussions should still focus on guideline-based vaccination, sleep, nutrition, exercise, chronic-disease management, and clinician evaluation of recurrent infections or immune suppression.
    • Evidence-map change: No material change. Kept as biomarker/watchlist context for infection resilience and geroscience, not a treatment target.

    Evidence-map / watchlist disposition

    • No core intervention changed confidence level this week.
    • Exercise remains the strongest healthspan platform; new MCI exercise meta-analyses add cognitive/mood modality nuance but do not establish a specific biomarker-driven dose target.
    • Vitamin D evidence supports clinician-guided detection and correction of severe deficiency, not high-dose supplementation as an anti-aging strategy.
    • Retatrutide strengthens disease-specific incretin evidence for obesity plus type 2 diabetes but remains a medication requiring indication-based clinician oversight, especially in older adults where lean mass, hydration, orthostasis, and fall risk matter.
    • Immune-aging findings are biologically important but remain biomarker/watchlist evidence rather than actionable treatment guidance.
