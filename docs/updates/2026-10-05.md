## Accepted items

### 1) Structured exercise and epicardial adipose volume in community-dwelling older adults

- **Citation / URL:** Xue X, Chen S, Cheng Y, Li Q, Ye Z, Li H, et al. *A 12-month structured exercise reduces epicardial adipose volume in a frequency-dependent manner and lowers serum M-CSF in community-dwelling older adults: a randomized controlled trial.* Frontiers in Public Health. 2026. DOI: [10.3389/fpubh.2026.1938784](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1938784). PMID: [42819224](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42819224/).
- **Date found:** 2026-10-05.
- **Evidence type:** Pragmatic randomized controlled trial.
- **Population:** 156 community-dwelling adults aged ≥60; 118 completed 12-month follow-up; mean age about 69 years; 46.6% men among completers.
- **Rubric score:** 8.2/10.
- **Summary:** Participants were assigned to health education alone or a structured exercise program with one supervised center-based session plus at least two matched home-based sessions weekly for 12 months. Epicardial adipose volume, body-fat measures, and inflammatory markers were measured before and after the intervention.
- **Main finding:** Structured exercise reduced epicardial adipose volume versus control by about 15 mL, and each additional weekly exercise session was associated with greater epicardial-fat reduction. Serum M-CSF was the only inflammatory marker with a significant adjusted between-group difference.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** Reinforces exercise as a cardiometabolic-healthspan intervention with an imaging marker linked to cardiac/metabolic risk. It is relevant to older men because the intervention is non-pharmacologic, lasted a full year, and included community-dwelling older adults rather than only young/athletic volunteers.
- **Limitations:** Single-center trial; surrogate endpoint rather than cardiovascular events, mortality, frailty, or hospitalization; completer sample was smaller than randomized sample; exact transferability to a 74-year-old with orthopedic, cardiac, balance, or frailty limitations depends on clinical context.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** Exercise dose and progression should be individualized. A clinician or qualified exercise professional is especially important for chest pain, exertional breathlessness, dizziness, falls risk, severe osteoporosis, neuropathy, uncontrolled blood pressure, recent cardiac events, or medication-related hypoglycemia/orthostasis.
- **Evidence-map change:** Minor. Adds cardiometabolic imaging support to the already-strong exercise category without changing the core recommendation confidence.

### 2) Intrinsic capacity and frailty index for disability and mortality risk

- **Citation / URL:** Feng B, Wang J, Li Y, Ma L, Yin Y, Xu J, et al. *Independent and joint associations of intrinsic capacity and frailty index with incident disability and all-cause mortality among older Chinese adults.* Frontiers in Public Health. 2026. DOI: [10.3389/fpubh.2026.1859572](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1859572). PMID: [42818694](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42818694/).
- **Date found:** 2026-10-05.
- **Evidence type:** Prospective cohort analysis.
- **Population:** 7,168 adults aged ≥60 from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey.
- **Rubric score:** 8.3/10.
- **Summary:** The study compared intrinsic capacity, measured across mobility, vitality, cognition, psychological, and sensory domains, with a deficit-accumulation frailty index for prediction of incident activities-of-daily-living disability and all-cause mortality.
- **Main finding:** Higher intrinsic capacity was associated with lower odds of incident disability and lower mortality risk, while higher frailty index was associated with higher disability and mortality risk. People with both lower intrinsic capacity and higher frailty had the highest risk profile.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** Supports tracking multidomain function, not just disease labels or biomarkers. Mobility, vitality, cognition, mood, sensory function, and accumulated deficits can identify risks that standard cardiometabolic labs may miss.
- **Limitations:** Observational design; China-based cohort; associations may reflect underlying illness rather than causal effects of changing an index score; implementation in routine care requires practical clinical tools.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** Frailty or intrinsic-capacity scores are screening/risk-stratification tools, not stand-alone diagnoses. New functional decline warrants clinician review for reversible contributors such as medication adverse effects, anemia, endocrine disease, malnutrition, depression, sleep disorders, infection, cardiac disease, neurologic disease, or sensory impairment.
- **Evidence-map change:** Yes. The diagnostics/tracking section now emphasizes combined intrinsic-capacity plus frailty assessment for disability/mortality risk stratification.

### 3) Gait speed and survival in adults aged 90 and older

- **Citation / URL:** Alam R, Colbeth HL, Posis AIB, George KM, Colcord K, Meunier CC, et al. *Gait Speed and Survival in Those With Exceptional Longevity: The LifeAfter90 Study.* Journal of the American Geriatrics Society. 2026 Sep 21. DOI: [10.1111/jgs.70716](https://doi.org/10.1111/jgs.70716). PMID: [42768272](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42768272/).
- **Date found:** 2026-10-05.
- **Evidence type:** Prospective cohort study.
- **Population:** 502 ethnoracially diverse adults aged ≥90; mean age 92.9 years; 62.6% women; average follow-up 3.6 years.
- **Rubric score:** 8.0/10.
- **Summary:** Baseline 4-meter gait speed was tested as a predictor of all-cause mortality in the LifeAfter90 cohort.
- **Main finding:** Each 0.21 m/s higher gait speed was associated with about 20% lower mortality risk after demographic adjustment. The association was strongest in the 90–94 age group and attenuated among participants aged ≥95.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** Gait speed is a simple, low-cost functional vital sign. Although the study is in adults older than 90, it strengthens the case for measuring and protecting walking speed before very old age.
- **Limitations:** Observational; older-than-target cohort; gait speed may be a marker of underlying health rather than a direct causal target; not an exercise trial; mortality association weakened in the 95+ subgroup.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** Slow or worsening gait speed should trigger evaluation for reversible causes and fall risk rather than self-directed aggressive training. Assessment should consider pain, neuropathy, vestibular problems, vision, orthostatic hypotension, medication effects, cardiovascular symptoms, and neurologic signs.
- **Evidence-map change:** Minor. Reinforces gait speed and simple physical-performance measures as practical risk markers.

### 4) BNP and intrinsic-capacity decline in older adults

- **Citation / URL:** Zhang N, Qiu X, Wang X, Wei L, Li X, Xie S, et al. *Association of B-Type Natriuretic Peptide With Intrinsic Capacity Trajectories Among Chinese Older Adults.* Journal of the American Geriatrics Society. 2026 Sep 20. DOI: [10.1111/jgs.70715](https://doi.org/10.1111/jgs.70715). PMID: [42764591](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42764591/).
- **Date found:** 2026-10-05.
- **Evidence type:** Population-based prospective cohort study.
- **Population:** 1,494 Chinese older adults; mean age 77.9 years; 46.4% male; three waves over 4 years.
- **Rubric score:** 7.8/10.
- **Summary:** The study tested whether baseline and persistent BNP elevation were associated with lower intrinsic-capacity scores and faster decline across mobility, vitality, cognition, psychological, and sensory domains.
- **Main finding:** Higher BNP and persistent BNP abnormalities were associated with lower intrinsic capacity and greater intrinsic-capacity decline, including among participants without diagnosed cardiovascular disease.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** BNP is already used clinically in cardiovascular assessment. This study suggests cardiac stress markers may also flag broader functional vulnerability in older adults.
- **Limitations:** Observational; biomarker association does not prove BNP-lowering would preserve function; residual confounding and occult disease are possible; thresholds and actions need clinician interpretation.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** BNP should not be used as a consumer longevity target. Elevated or changing BNP requires clinician interpretation in context of heart failure, atrial fibrillation, kidney function, age, medications, symptoms, and volume status.
- **Evidence-map change:** Minor. Adds nuance to standard lab/risk-factor tracking and intrinsic-capacity assessment, but does not create a new intervention.

### 5) Live microbial interventions for cognition and muscle outcomes in aging adults

- **Citation / URL:** Silva-Olivares F, Vidal N, Acevedo-Hernández P, Rabbia V, Petermann-Rocha F, Thomas-Valdés S, et al. *Can live microbial interventions support healthy aging? A systematic review and meta-analysis of cognitive and muscle outcomes in aging adults.* Frontiers in Nutrition. 2026. DOI: [10.3389/fnut.2026.1869929](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2026.1869929). PMID: [42819170](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42819170/).
- **Date found:** 2026-10-05.
- **Evidence type:** Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized placebo-controlled trials.
- **Population:** 24 trials with 1,809 adults; trial populations had mean age ≥60; interventions were probiotics or synbiotics, with no eligible fecal microbiota transplantation trials.
- **Rubric score:** 7.6/10, included as a cautious watchlist item because of public interest and explicit uncertainty.
- **Summary:** The review synthesized RCT evidence on live microorganism-based interventions for cognitive and muscle-related outcomes in older adults.
- **Main finding:** Some short-term cognitive and handgrip signals were positive, but heterogeneity was high, follow-up was short, muscle evidence was sparse, and results were sensitive to individual studies. The authors concluded that firm efficacy claims are not warranted.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** Gut-brain-muscle interventions are heavily marketed in healthy-aging circles. This review is useful mainly because it separates preliminary signals from clinically reliable evidence.
- **Limitations:** Small, heterogeneous trials; high statistical heterogeneity for MMSE; post hoc subgroup findings; few muscle-outcome trials; no hard outcomes such as dementia incidence, falls, hospitalization, disability, or mortality.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** Probiotic/synbiotic products vary widely. Discuss use with a clinician if immunocompromised, frail, recently hospitalized, using central lines, taking immunosuppressive therapy, or managing severe GI disease. This is not evidence for fecal microbiota transplantation or for replacing diet, exercise, sleep, or standard medical care.
- **Evidence-map change:** No material confidence change. Added to watchlist framing only: promising but low-certainty, product-specific, and not treatment-directing.

### 6) Intravenous NAD+ and NAD+ precursors in wellness settings

- **Citation / URL:** Alangari A, Arif J, Al Qureshah F, Alkhodairy F, et al. *Narrative review of intravenous NAD+ and NAD+ precursors in wellness and translational medicine.* Frontiers in Aging. 2026. DOI: [10.3389/fragi.2026.1887175](https://doi.org/10.3389/fragi.2026.1887175). PMID: [42787547](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42787547/).
- **Date found:** 2026-10-05.
- **Evidence type:** Narrative review of human IV NAD+ / IV NAD+ precursor evidence and mechanistic/safety context.
- **Population:** Human clinical reports from January 2018 to March 2026, largely small uncontrolled studies, observational investigations, and case reports.
- **Rubric score:** 7.5/10 as a cautionary geroscience/watchlist item, not as efficacy evidence.
- **Summary:** The review assessed IV NAD+ and IV NAD+ precursor use in wellness and translational medicine, where commercial claims have outpaced human outcome data.
- **Main finding:** Human evidence remains sparse and preliminary. Short-term IV NAD+ or NMN may change circulating NAD+ and selected biomarkers, but clinical significance, durability, optimal dosing, and long-term safety are unresolved. Reported adverse effects include nausea, cramping, flushing, and chest discomfort.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** NAD-boosting infusions are marketed for anti-aging, fatigue, sleep, and neurologic wellness. This review supports a cautious stance: biomarker movement is not healthspan benefit.
- **Limitations:** Narrative review rather than systematic meta-analysis; evidence base mostly small, uncontrolled, short follow-up studies; limited older-adult-specific outcomes; wellness-clinic protocols may differ from studies.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** IV therapies can carry infusion reactions, vascular-access complications, fluid/electrolyte issues, medication/comorbidity interactions, and opportunity cost. This is not a recommendation to use IV NAD+; discuss any contemplated infusion with a qualified clinician, especially with cardiovascular disease, arrhythmia, kidney disease, cancer history, polypharmacy, or frailty.
- **Evidence-map change:** Yes. The NAD watchlist now explicitly distinguishes IV NAD+/precursor wellness use from outcome-proven geroscience intervention.

## Evidence-map / watchlist disposition

- Exercise confidence remains strong; this week adds an older-adult RCT showing reduced epicardial adipose volume, a cardiometabolic surrogate, after a year of structured exercise.
- Functional tracking confidence increased slightly: intrinsic capacity, frailty index, gait speed, and BNP-contextualized functional decline all point toward multidomain function assessment as a clinician-discussion tool.
- Probiotics/synbiotics for cognition or muscle remain low-certainty and product-specific; no evidence-map upgrade was made.
- IV NAD+ / NAD+ precursor therapy remains a cautionary geroscience watchlist topic, with insufficient evidence for routine wellness or longevity use and unresolved long-term safety.
