## Accepted items

### 1) Multifactorial and multiple-component fall prevention in community-dwelling older adults

- **Citation / URL:** Swarnalatha C, Wood L, Newell P, Clark CE, Adedire O, Clemson L, et al. *Multifactorial and multiple component interventions for preventing falls in older people living in the community.* Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. 2026 Sep 21. DOI: [10.1002/14651858.CD012221.pub3](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012221.pub3). PMID: [42764178](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42764178/).
- **Date found:** 2026-09-27.
- **Evidence type:** Cochrane systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.
- **Population:** Community-dwelling adults aged ≥60; 110 RCTs with 48,919 participants.
- **Rubric score:** 9.3/10.
- **Summary:** This update compared individualized multifactorial fall-prevention programs and fixed multiple-component programs against usual care, exercise, and falls advice/education.
- **Main finding:** Multifactorial interventions may reduce falls rate versus usual care (rate ratio 0.76) and recurrent-fall risk (RR 0.87), but may have little or no effect on the proportion of people who fall. Multiple-component interventions showed smaller or uncertain effects, and comparisons against exercise or falls education often showed little added benefit or very uncertain evidence.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** Falls are a major pathway to fracture, hospitalization, disability, and loss of independence. The review supports individualized fall-risk assessment and targeted remediation while preserving the central role of exercise/balance work.
- **Limitations:** Certainty was often low because of risk of bias, heterogeneity, and possible publication bias; effects vary by baseline risk, intervention components, adherence, and comparator.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** Fall-prevention plans should be individualized with a clinician, physical therapist, or qualified exercise professional when there is dizziness, neuropathy, orthostatic hypotension, cardiac symptoms, osteoporosis, sedating medication use, or prior falls. This is not a recommendation to start unsupervised challenging balance exercise.
- **Evidence-map change:** Yes. The balance/fall-prevention entry was strengthened and made more nuanced: individualized multifactorial programs may reduce fall rate/recurrent falls, but they are not clearly superior to exercise or education in all comparisons.

### 2) Life’s Essential 8 cardiovascular health and unhealthy-aging trajectories

- **Citation / URL:** Gómez-Ángel D, Sotos-Prieto M, García-Esquinas E, Carballo-Casla A, Peñalvo JL, Rodríguez-Artalejo F, et al. *Cardiovascular health, as per life's essential 8, and unhealthy aging trajectories.* GeroScience. 2026 Sep 23. DOI: [10.1007/s11357-026-02553-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11357-026-02553-9). PMID: [42776389](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42776389/).
- **Date found:** 2026-09-27.
- **Evidence type:** Prospective older-adult cohort with repeated cardiovascular-health and aging-trajectory measures.
- **Population:** 2,487 adults aged ≥65 in the Seniors-ENRICA-2 cohort in Madrid, followed for 5.2 years.
- **Rubric score:** 8.4/10.
- **Summary:** The study evaluated whether American Heart Association Life’s Essential 8 cardiovascular-health scores related to deficit accumulation and intrinsic-capacity impairment over time.
- **Main finding:** Each 10-point higher Life’s Essential 8 score was associated with lower baseline deficit accumulation and intrinsic-capacity impairment in both women and men, and with a slower annual increase in deficit accumulation. Physical activity, sleep health, BMI, and blood glucose were prominent contributors.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** The population is directly older-adult relevant and supports treating cardiovascular health as a multidomain healthspan platform rather than a narrow heart-disease topic.
- **Limitations:** Observational design; Spain-based cohort; associations do not prove that changing a single Life’s Essential 8 component will slow aging trajectories; residual confounding and reverse causation are possible.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** Discuss blood pressure, lipids, glucose, sleep, weight, and exercise targets with clinicians, especially when medications, orthostatic symptoms, sleep apnea, frailty, kidney disease, or sarcopenia risk are present.
- **Evidence-map change:** Minor. Reinforces strong confidence in standard cardiometabolic risk-factor management and supports linking sleep/physical activity/metabolic markers to function-preservation goals.

### 3) Guideline-directed medical therapy in older adults with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction

- **Citation / URL:** Tan E, Meei-Wah C, Teo YH, Tan LF, Ho VWT, Lin W, et al. *Survival benefit of guideline-directed medical therapy in older patients with heart failure: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* Heart. 2026 Sep 24. DOI: [10.1136/heartjnl-2025-326305](https://doi.org/10.1136/heartjnl-2025-326305). PMID: [41672763](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41672763/).
- **Date found:** 2026-09-27.
- **Evidence type:** Systematic review and meta-analysis.
- **Population:** 34 studies with 92,916 adults aged ≥65 with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction / left ventricular systolic dysfunction.
- **Rubric score:** 8.5/10 for indicated cardiovascular-disease management; not a general longevity-drug item.
- **Summary:** The review examined survival, safety, and frailty-related implementation of guideline-directed heart-failure therapies in older adults.
- **Main finding:** GDMT use in older adults with left ventricular systolic dysfunction was associated with lower mortality (HR 0.69), but frail older adults were less likely to receive GDMT and beta-blockers were associated with more bradycardia. Data on full four-class GDMT in older/frail patients were lacking.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** Heart failure is common with aging and strongly affects function, hospitalization risk, and survival. For someone with HFrEF, evidence-based heart-failure optimization is more outcome-relevant than speculative geroscience drugs.
- **Limitations:** Included studies varied in design, era, drug combinations, frailty definitions, and confounding control; frail patients remain underrepresented; evidence on complete contemporary four-class therapy in older/frail adults is limited.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** This is a clinician-managed, diagnosis-specific topic. Medication choices and titration require monitoring for heart rate, blood pressure, renal function, potassium, volume status, dizziness/falls, and drug interactions. Do not start, stop, or change heart-failure medications without a treating clinician.
- **Evidence-map change:** Minor. Reinforces disease-specific cardiometabolic/cardiovascular optimization and frailty-aware medication monitoring rather than adding a new longevity intervention.

### 4) Frailty trajectories and cardiovascular disease in early cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic risk states

- **Citation / URL:** Zhang Y, Liu A, Shi C, Xu Y, Yang R, An Z, et al. *Frailty trajectories and cardiovascular disease in adults with CKM stages 0-3.* GeroScience. 2026 Sep 24. DOI: [10.1007/s11357-026-02559-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11357-026-02559-3). PMID: [42778867](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42778867/).
- **Date found:** 2026-09-27.
- **Evidence type:** Longitudinal cohort analysis.
- **Population:** 4,219 adults aged ≥45 with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic stages 0-3 from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study.
- **Rubric score:** 8.0/10.
- **Summary:** The study tested whether baseline frailty and repeated frailty-index trajectories predicted later cardiovascular disease among adults with early CKM risk.
- **Main finding:** Compared with low-stable frailty trajectories, moderate-gradual and high-rapid-rise frailty trajectories were associated with higher odds of CVD (OR 1.87 and 3.52). The high-rapid-rise group showed a large stroke association (OR 4.98). Baseline prefrailty/frailty also predicted incident CVD.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** Supports repeated frailty/function assessment as a complement to standard cardiometabolic markers, especially where mobility, strength, weight change, medication burden, or stroke risk are concerns.
- **Limitations:** Observational; cohort was Chinese and included adults younger than 74; self-reported CVD outcomes may misclassify events; frailty-index changes may reflect early disease rather than cause it.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** Frailty screening is risk stratification, not a stand-alone diagnosis or treatment plan. New or rapidly worsening frailty should prompt clinician review for reversible contributors such as medication effects, anemia, endocrine disease, nutrition problems, depression, sleep disorders, infection, cardiovascular disease, or neurologic disease.
- **Evidence-map change:** Minor. Reinforces intrinsic-capacity/frailty tracking as a cardiometabolic risk-context tool.

### 5) Dietetic counseling plus digital tools for metabolic-syndrome risk

- **Citation / URL:** Makri R, Evangelou I, Karaglani E, Vlachava M, Dafoulas GE, Drympeta I, et al. *Impact of Dietetic Counseling Combined With Digital Tools on Obesity-Related Indices in Greek Adults: The GATEKEEPER Study.* Obesity. 2026 Oct. DOI: [10.1002/oby.70284](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.70284). PMID: [42759999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42759999/).
- **Date found:** 2026-09-27.
- **Evidence type:** Randomized controlled trial.
- **Population:** 984 adults aged ≥55 with at least one metabolic-syndrome risk factor; 954 included in analysis; 3-month intervention.
- **Rubric score:** 7.8/10.
- **Summary:** Participants received monthly dietetic counseling alone, counseling plus a web platform, or counseling plus platform and digital devices.
- **Main finding:** Dietetic counseling improved waist circumference, BMI/body-composition indices, and glycemic markers across groups. Digital tools increased physical activity modestly but did not add short-term metabolic benefit and were associated with smaller waist reductions than counseling alone.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** Supports clinician/dietitian-led metabolic-risk counseling as the active ingredient; digital tools may help activity engagement but should not be assumed to improve metabolic outcomes by themselves.
- **Limitations:** Short 3-month follow-up; adults ≥55 rather than specifically ≥70; unclear durability, clinical-event impact, and applicability to frail or sarcopenic older adults; digital adherence and usability may differ by individual.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** Weight-loss or waist-reduction efforts in older adults should protect protein intake, resistance training, bone health, hydration, and functional status. Avoid aggressive unsupervised dieting, especially with diabetes medications, kidney disease, heart failure, frailty, or unintended weight loss.
- **Evidence-map change:** No material change. Reinforces existing cardiometabolic/nutrition confidence and the need to protect lean mass during weight-focused interventions.

## Evidence-map / watchlist disposition

- Balance/fall-prevention confidence was updated with Cochrane nuance: individualized multifactorial programs can reduce fall rate/recurrent falls versus usual care, but certainty is often low and they are not clearly superior to exercise or education in all settings.
- Cardiometabolic risk management remains high confidence; the new Life’s Essential 8 and CKM/frailty cohort studies support multidomain tracking of activity, sleep, metabolic markers, and function.
- Disease-specific medication optimization, such as HFrEF guideline-directed therapy, remains clinician-managed and indication-specific, with frailty and adverse-effect monitoring central in older adults.
- No geroscience watchlist compound gained enough human outcome evidence this week to change its status.
