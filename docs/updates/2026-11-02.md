## Accepted items

### 1) Low-dose aspirin did not preserve multidomain intrinsic capacity in ASPREE

- **Citation / URL:** Ryan J, Phyo AZZ, Wieczorek M, et al. *Validation of an intrinsic capacity index and the effects of aspirin in older males and females: secondary analysis of a randomized controlled trial.* GeroScience. Published online 2026 Oct 29. DOI: [10.1007/s11357-026-02624-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11357-026-02624-x). PMID: [42955660](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42955660/).
- **Date found:** 2026-11-02.
- **Evidence type:** Post hoc secondary analysis of the randomized, placebo-controlled ASPREE trial with repeated five-year assessment.
- **Population:** 18,581 community-dwelling adults aged ≥65 without the trial's major baseline disease/disability exclusions; results were analyzed by sex.
- **Rubric score:** **8.8/10**.
- **Summary:** Investigators validated a four-domain intrinsic-capacity index covering cognition, locomotion, vitality, and psychological well-being, then tested whether daily 100 mg aspirin altered its trajectory.
- **Main finding:** Lower baseline intrinsic capacity strongly identified lower dementia- and disability-free survival, but aspirin did not slow decline. No aspirin effect was found in males; females assigned aspirin had a statistically detectable but very small additional decline, with only borderline evidence that the treatment effect differed by sex.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** This directly argues against treating low-dose aspirin as a general function-preserving or anti-inflammatory longevity intervention in otherwise healthy older men. Aspirin may still have standard cardiovascular or cerebrovascular indications in selected people.
- **Limitations:** Post hoc outcome and sex analyses; the intrinsic-capacity index omitted sensory domains; the female effect was small; ASPREE eligibility limits generalization to less healthy or previously diseased populations.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** This is not a reason to start or stop aspirin independently. Aspirin decisions require clinician review of the actual indication and competing bleeding, cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, kidney, medication-interaction, and procedural risks.
- **Evidence-map change:** **Yes—caution strengthened.** Low-dose aspirin does not have evidence as a general healthy-aging or function-preservation therapy.

### 2) GLP-1 cardiovascular benefit appears broadly preserved after age 65, but ≥75 evidence remains thinner

- **Citation / URL:** Fujii A, Hasebe M, Furuichi K, Yabe D, Yoshiji S. *Cardiovascular Effects of GLP-1 Receptor Agonists Across Age Groups: An Updated Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.* Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism. Published online 2026 Oct 27. DOI: [10.1111/dom.71496](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.71496). PMID: [42946897](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42946897/).
- **Date found:** 2026-11-02.
- **Evidence type:** Random-effects meta-analysis of eight placebo-controlled cardiovascular-outcome trials.
- **Population:** 71,926 adults with type 2 diabetes or overweight/obesity; age-stratified results compared participants aged <65 and ≥65, with limited data for ≥75.
- **Rubric score:** **8.6/10**.
- **Summary:** The analysis pooled major adverse cardiovascular-event results from SUSTAIN-6, EXSCEL, Harmony Outcomes, PIONEER 6, REWIND, AMPLITUDE-O, SELECT, and SOUL.
- **Main finding:** GLP-1 receptor agonists reduced major cardiovascular events in both age groups (pooled HR 0.81 for <65 and 0.84 for ≥65), with no clear evidence that relative benefit differed by age. Only four trials reported a ≥75 stratum, and that group was comparatively small.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** Age alone does not appear to eliminate disease-specific cardiovascular benefit when a GLP-1 medicine is otherwise indicated. It does not establish general longevity benefit or resolve older-adult tolerability, muscle, nutrition, bone, or functional concerns.
- **Limitations:** Trial-level age subgroups rather than individual-participant analysis; heterogeneous trials and drugs; limited ≥75 representation; relative effects do not show each person's absolute benefit or treatment burden.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** Decisions remain indication-specific. Older-adult discussion may include gastrointestinal effects, hydration, kidney function, gallbladder/pancreatitis concerns, retinopathy where relevant, excessive or rapid weight loss, protein/energy adequacy, muscle strength, falls, and fracture risk.
- **Evidence-map change:** **Yes—minor strengthening.** Cardiovascular efficacy appears preserved after 65, while geriatric safety and function still require individualized monitoring.

### 3) SGLT2 inhibitor safety in adults aged ≥75: reassuring active-comparator cohort, with imprecision for uncommon outcomes

- **Citation / URL:** Harbi H, Nesrallah M, Carney G, et al. *Safety of Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter 2 Inhibitors in Older Adults With Type 2 Diabetes: A Cohort Study.* Diabetes Care. Published online 2026 Oct 27. DOI: [10.2337/dc26-1316](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc26-1316). PMID: [42937517](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42937517/).
- **Date found:** 2026-11-02.
- **Evidence type:** Population-based active-comparator, new-user cohort with propensity-score weighting.
- **Population:** 6,286 Canadian adults aged ≥75 with type 2 diabetes starting an SGLT2 inhibitor or DPP-4 inhibitor; median ages were 79 and 82, respectively.
- **Rubric score:** **8.3/10**.
- **Summary:** The study compared infection, ketoacidosis, acute kidney injury, falls, fractures, and all-cause hospitalization during routine care.
- **Main finding:** SGLT2 initiation was associated with lower acute-kidney-injury and all-cause-hospitalization rates and no detected infection increase versus DPP-4 initiation. Falls, fractures, and ketoacidosis did not differ statistically, but confidence intervals were compatible with benefit or harm for some outcomes.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** This adds near-age-matched safety evidence for indicated diabetes/cardiorenal use and argues against excluding SGLT2 inhibitors solely because a person is entering the ≥75 age range.
- **Limitations:** Observational residual confounding; comparator groups differed in age and sex before weighting; administrative outcome definitions; uncommon-event estimates were imprecise; results do not apply to people without a standard indication.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** This is not evidence for general anti-aging use. Clinician monitoring may include volume status, blood pressure/orthostasis, kidney function, genital infection, ketoacidosis risk during illness or reduced intake, and peri-procedural/sick-day plans.
- **Evidence-map change:** **Yes—minor strengthening for indicated use.** Reassuring real-world safety evidence now extends specifically to adults ≥75, without removing standard geriatric precautions.

### 4) Digital balance training improves balance measures, but is not superior to conventional exercise and has no direct falls evidence

- **Citation / URL:** Li F, Li X, Li Q, et al. *Comparative Effectiveness of Digital Exercise-Based Interventions for Improving Balance in Older Adults: Systematic Review and Network Meta-Analysis With GRADE Certainty of Evidence.* JMIR Aging. 2026;9:e101231. DOI: [10.2196/101231](https://doi.org/10.2196/101231). PMID: [42954170](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42954170/).
- **Date found:** 2026-11-02.
- **Evidence type:** Systematic review and network meta-analysis of 60 randomized trials, with GRADE assessment.
- **Population:** 3,469 older adults; mean age 73.1 years across included trials.
- **Rubric score:** **8.2/10**.
- **Summary:** Telerehabilitation, balance-platform exergaming, motion-capture exergaming, other games, wearables, conventional exercise, and no treatment were compared for balance, Timed Up and Go, and gait speed.
- **Main finding:** Telerehabilitation and two exergaming categories improved some balance outcomes versus no treatment, but no digital intervention outperformed conventional exercise. Most comparisons were low or very-low certainty, and no trial reported falls.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** Digital options may improve access, adherence, or home practice when conventional supervised programs are difficult to attend, but they should not be assumed to prevent falls or replace an appropriately designed exercise program.
- **Limitations:** Sparse networks for some technologies; mostly low-certainty evidence; variable comparator intensity; short follow-up; balance-test outcomes rather than falls, injuries, independence, or hospitalization.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** Home technology should account for fall risk, vision/hearing, neuropathy, vestibular disease, cognition, space and equipment hazards, and whether initial supervision or physical-therapy assessment is needed.
- **Evidence-map change:** **Yes—implementation refinement.** Digital balance exercise is a potentially useful delivery route, not a superior intervention and not yet direct fall-prevention evidence.

### 5) Frailty review confirms exercise benefit; added nutritional supplementation remains uncertain

- **Citation / URL:** Johannesson J, Lood Q, Rothenberg E, Andersson Hammar I. *The impact of interventions combining nutrition and physical exercise on frailty in old age: A systematic literature review and Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM).* Nutrition and Health. Published online 2026 Oct 28. DOI: [10.1177/02601060261499544](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261499544). PMID: [42949507](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42949507/).
- **Date found:** 2026-11-02.
- **Evidence type:** Systematic review and SWiM narrative synthesis of randomized trials, with RoB 2 and GRADE.
- **Population:** Community-dwelling adults aged ≥65 who were explicitly classified as frail; 10 publications met eligibility criteria.
- **Rubric score:** **8.0/10**.
- **Summary:** The review focused on interventions combining exercise and nutrition and asked whether they improved frailty status and related function.
- **Main finding:** Structured exercise consistently improved frailty-related outcomes. Some combined programs reported frailty reversal, mobility, lean-mass, or performance benefits, but the evidence could not determine whether nutrition added benefit beyond exercise alone.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** Exercise remains the better-supported core of frailty management. Nutrition should be individualized to documented intake, weight change, protein-energy needs, deficiencies, disease, and swallowing/dental context rather than added automatically as a supplement package.
- **Limitations:** Few trials; small samples; heterogeneous frailty definitions, interventions, supplements, and outcomes; no pooled effect; certainty ranged from low to high by result.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** Frailty can increase exercise, fall, and recovery risks. Major exercise or nutrition changes warrant clinician and qualified exercise/dietetic input, particularly with kidney disease, heart failure, diabetes, osteoporosis, unintentional weight loss, or polypharmacy.
- **Evidence-map change:** No confidence-level change. Reinforces exercise-centered multidomain support while leaving the incremental value of nutritional supplementation uncertain.

### 6) Blood-flow-restriction resistance training: strength benefit with acute blood-pressure elevations

- **Citation / URL:** Norouzi K, Nobari H, Jamali Masoomi S, et al. *Acute and Chronic Cardiovascular and Neuromuscular Effects of Blood Flow Restriction Resistance Training in Older Adults: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.* Biological Research for Nursing. Published online 2026 Oct 28. DOI: [10.1177/10998004261502747](https://doi.org/10.1177/10998004261502747). PMID: [42950254](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42950254/).
- **Date found:** 2026-11-02.
- **Evidence type:** Systematic review and meta-analysis of 27 randomized parallel or crossover studies.
- **Population:** Older adults performing low-load resistance exercise with blood-flow restriction.
- **Rubric score:** **8.1/10** as an exercise-safety/refinement item.
- **Summary:** The review separated acute hemodynamic responses from longer-term strength, muscle-size, vascular, and resting cardiovascular outcomes.
- **Main finding:** Compared with no exercise, blood-flow-restriction training improved selected lower-limb strength measures and quadriceps size. Acutely, systolic pressure was higher than no exercise, while diastolic and mean arterial pressures were higher than during conventional resistance training; comparisons with conventional training for neuromuscular outcomes were mixed.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** It may be an alternative when heavy loading is not feasible, but it is not proven cardiovascularly safer or functionally superior to conventional progressive resistance training.
- **Limitations:** Small heterogeneous studies; mixed protocols and comparators; selected physiological and muscle outcomes rather than falls, disability, hospitalization, or long-term adverse events.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** Blood-flow restriction should not be treated as a DIY substitute for ordinary resistance training. Vascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, thrombosis history/risk, anticoagulation, neuropathy, wound/skin issues, and cardiovascular symptoms warrant clinician and appropriately trained exercise-professional review.
- **Evidence-map change:** **Yes—minor refinement.** Retain as a supervised alternative for selected people, with explicit acute hemodynamic caution and no superiority claim.

### 7) Older men can have substantial fragility-fracture burden despite lower DXA-defined osteoporosis prevalence

- **Citation / URL:** Lee YY, Huang YC, Kao SW, Chen YC, Cheng MT. *The osteoporosis-fracture paradox in older Taiwanese men: lower osteoporosis prevalence but comparable fragility fracture burden.* Archives of Osteoporosis. 2026;21:156. DOI: [10.1007/s11657-026-01782-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11657-026-01782-x). PMID: [42965625](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42965625/).
- **Date found:** 2026-11-02.
- **Evidence type:** Retrospective, age-matched clinical DXA cohort.
- **Population:** 2,031 Taiwanese men undergoing clinical DXA in 2021-2023, matched 1:1 to women of the same age.
- **Rubric score:** **7.8/10**.
- **Summary:** The study compared DXA-defined osteoporosis with radiographic and medical-record evidence of vertebral and hip fractures.
- **Main finding:** Osteoporosis prevalence was lower in men than women (32.4% versus 51.4%), but vertebral-fracture prevalence was almost identical and hip-fracture prevalence did not differ significantly. Men had more moderate-to-severe vertebral fractures in sensitivity analysis, and male sex remained associated with fractures after adjustment.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** A non-osteoporotic DXA result does not by itself exclude clinically important fracture risk. Prior fragility fracture, falls, medications, vertebral imaging when indicated, and other clinical risk factors remain relevant.
- **Limitations:** Selected clinical DXA population; retrospective design; Taiwanese setting; possible referral and ascertainment bias; residual confounding; prevalence rather than prospective fracture incidence.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** This does not define a personal need for imaging or medication. Clinician discussion is especially relevant after height loss, back pain, a low-trauma fracture, recurrent falls, long-term glucocorticoid exposure, androgen-deprivation therapy, or other recognized risk factors.
- **Evidence-map change:** **Yes—clarification.** Bone-health assessment in older men should integrate DXA with fracture history and clinical risk rather than relying on the T-score alone.

### 8) Orthostatic blood-pressure recovery time may identify fall risk better than a single standing threshold

- **Citation / URL:** Alvarez VA, Hertneck C, Rothenbacher D, Denkinger M, Dallmeier D. *Orthostatic blood pressure fluctuations and risk of falls in community-dwelling older adults: the role of recovery time.* BMC Geriatrics. 2026;26. DOI: [10.1186/s12877-026-08516-1](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-08516-1). PMID: [42958062](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42958062/).
- **Date found:** 2026-11-02.
- **Evidence type:** Prospective cohort analysis with repeated blood-pressure measurements after standing and 12-month falls ascertainment.
- **Population:** 509 community-dwelling older adults; mean age 79 years, with 168 falls recorded.
- **Rubric score:** **8.1/10**.
- **Summary:** Blood pressure was measured supine, immediately after standing, and at one, two, and three minutes; analyses distinguished early, delayed, and absent recovery.
- **Main finding:** Conventional overall orthostatic hypotension was not associated with falls, but systolic orthostatic hypotension was. Delayed or absent blood-pressure recovery identified substantially higher fall odds, although estimates had wide confidence intervals.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** When dizziness, near-falls, falls, dehydration, or blood-pressure-treatment concerns are present, the pattern and recovery of standing blood pressure may be more informative than one reading alone.
- **Limitations:** Modest cohort and event counts; observational residual confounding; wide confidence intervals; measurements from a research protocol may not map directly to routine home devices; association does not prove that altering recovery time prevents falls.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** Do not change blood-pressure, diuretic, prostate, sedative, or other medicines independently. Clinician review may include standardized seated/supine and standing measurements, symptoms, hydration, anemia, autonomic disease, medication timing, and broader multifactorial fall assessment.
- **Evidence-map change:** **Yes—risk-assessment refinement.** Orthostatic recovery time is a useful clinician-discussion detail, not a stand-alone diagnosis or treatment target.

### 9) Enhanced influenza vaccines: large pragmatic-trial evidence supports benefit in older adults, with local-guideline dependence

- **Citation / URL:** Callens S, Peeters M, Van Laethem Y. *Recent trial evidence supports enhanced influenza vaccines for older adults: implications for Belgian practice.* Acta Clinica Belgica. Published online 2026 Oct 30. DOI: [10.1080/17843286.2026.2756252](https://doi.org/10.1080/17843286.2026.2756252). PMID: [42961083](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42961083/).
- **Date found:** 2026-11-02.
- **Evidence type:** Clinical narrative review of randomized, pragmatic, observational, and meta-analytic evidence, linked to national guidance.
- **Population:** Adults aged ≥65, with particular attention to high-dose and adjuvanted influenza vaccines and a pooled European pragmatic-trial population of 466,320.
- **Rubric score:** **7.8/10**.
- **Summary:** The review translates recent enhanced-versus-standard-dose influenza vaccine evidence into Belgian practice.
- **Main finding:** The pooled DANFLU-2/GALFLU analysis reported 31.9% relative vaccine effectiveness for high-dose versus standard-dose vaccine against laboratory-confirmed influenza hospitalization. Randomized evidence was stronger for high-dose than for adjuvanted vaccine; Belgian guidance gives stronger preference at age ≥75 than at 65-74.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** Influenza vaccination remains a high-confidence infection-resilience intervention, and enhanced formulations are a reasonable clinician-discussion topic as age and comorbidity increase risk. Exact products, eligibility, and preference vary by country and season.
- **Limitations:** Narrative rather than systematic review; product- and jurisdiction-specific recommendations; relative effectiveness does not state absolute benefit; pragmatic and observational estimates remain season- and strain-dependent.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** Vaccine selection and timing should follow current local clinical guidance, prior reaction history, availability, and co-administration plans. This evidence should not be used to substitute one jurisdiction's recommendation for another's.
- **Evidence-map change:** **Yes—minor strengthening.** Enhanced influenza formulations have outcome-based evidence in older adults, while recommendations remain local and season-specific.

### 10) Metformin and brain aging: small insulin-resistance RCT is promising but remains surrogate-heavy watchlist evidence

- **Citation / URL:** Pataky MW, Ruegsegger GN, Jo HJ, et al. *Metformin Preserves Regional Brain Glucose Uptake, Structure, and Functional Connectivity in Adults with Insulin Resistance.* Journal of Clinical Investigation. Published online 2026 Oct 27. DOI: [10.1172/JCI207388](https://doi.org/10.1172/JCI207388). PMID: [42944764](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42944764/).
- **Date found:** 2026-11-02.
- **Evidence type:** Small randomized placebo-controlled proof-of-concept trial embedded in a human imaging/mechanistic study, with supporting mouse experiments.
- **Population:** 72 adults underwent baseline characterization; 40 older adults with insulin resistance were randomized to 40 weeks of metformin or placebo.
- **Rubric score:** **7.4/10 — included as an important metformin watchlist item below the usual threshold.**
- **Summary:** PET, structural MRI, functional MRI, and cognitive testing were used to examine insulin-resistance-associated brain differences and whether metformin altered their progression.
- **Main finding:** Metformin improved systemic insulin sensitivity and was associated with preservation of selected regional glucose uptake, brain volume, functional connectivity, and a processing-speed/working-memory composite. The trial did not test dementia incidence, independence, quality of life, or survival.
- **Relevance to a 74-year-old male:** This provides a human randomized signal in older adults with insulin resistance, not evidence that metformin prevents dementia or extends healthspan in people without diabetes or another clinical indication.
- **Limitations:** Only 40 randomized participants; proof-of-concept design; multiple imaging and cognitive outcomes; short duration for neurodegenerative disease; selected insulin-resistant population; mechanistic mouse results do not increase the human clinical sample.
- **Safety / clinician cautions:** This does not support self-directed or off-label metformin use for brain aging. Clinician considerations include the actual metabolic indication, kidney function, gastrointestinal effects, vitamin B12, weight/appetite, acute-illness and procedural plans, and medication interactions.
- **Evidence-map change:** **Watchlist refinement only.** Human randomized evidence is more interesting, but remains too small and surrogate-focused to change metformin's early/uncertain longevity status.

## Evidence-map / watchlist disposition

- **Healthy-aging aspirin:** caution strengthened; low-dose aspirin did not preserve intrinsic capacity in older men and is not a general anti-aging intervention.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists:** cardiovascular relative benefit appears preserved after 65; ≥75 data and geriatric safety/function remain less certain.
- **SGLT2 inhibitors:** an active-comparator cohort in adults ≥75 is reassuring for indicated type 2 diabetes use, while standard volume, infection, ketoacidosis, kidney, and orthostatic precautions remain.
- **Exercise/frailty:** conventional exercise remains central. Digital balance tools can help delivery but have no direct falls evidence; nutrition's incremental benefit in combined frailty programs remains uncertain; blood-flow restriction is a supervised alternative rather than a superior or cardiovascularly safer default.
- **Bone health:** older men may carry substantial fracture burden even without DXA-defined osteoporosis, reinforcing clinical-risk and fracture-history assessment.
- **Falls/blood pressure:** delayed orthostatic recovery adds useful risk-assessment detail but does not justify self-adjusting medication.
- **Vaccines:** enhanced influenza vaccine evidence is strengthened, with product choice governed by current local recommendations.
- **Metformin:** a small insulin-resistance trial adds surrogate brain-imaging and cognition signals but does not establish dementia prevention, healthspan, or lifespan benefit.
